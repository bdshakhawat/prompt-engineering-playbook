Prompt Engineering Case Study
Case Study 1: Reproduce the Lecture's Metric-Generation Technique

Goal: Recreate the exact prompting technique used to generate evaluation metrics without generating solutions.

Prompt Used
"Give me five metrics to compare and contrast ways of accomplishing the following task. Don't list ways to accomplish the task.

How do I bake a cake in the woods on a fire with very limited cooking utensils? ----------"

Result (as reported in the lecture)

Five metrics: ease of preparation, cooking time, consistency of results, required supervision, risk of burning the cake.

Observation

The explicit instruction "don't list ways to accomplish the task," combined with a visual separator around the task description, successfully isolated the metric-generation step from solution-generation — the model produced pure evaluation dimensions rather than drifting into suggesting cooking methods.

Case Study 2: Apply the Same Metric-Generation Technique to a Physics Teaching Decision

Goal: Test whether this technique transfers cleanly to an educational context.

Prompt Used
"Give me five metrics to compare and contrast different ways of teaching a physics concept to high school students. Don't list ways to teach it.

The task is choosing how to introduce the concept of momentum conservation to a 10th grade class that has already covered force and Newton's Laws. ----------"

What to Evaluate
Did the model stay within the "metrics only" instruction, or did it drift into suggesting specific teaching methods despite the explicit instruction not to?
Are the resulting metrics genuinely useful and specific to physics instruction (e.g., "addresses common misconceptions directly"), or generic enough to apply to any teaching topic?
Key Takeaway

If the resulting metrics feel too generic, a useful refinement is to add more context about what matters specifically in physics instruction (e.g., "include at least one metric related to addressing common physics misconceptions") to sharpen the output.

Case Study 3: Test the Independence Effect — Same Conversation vs. Separate Conversation

Goal: Directly test the lecture's core claim that generating metrics in the same conversation as the solutions risks biased criteria.

Method A — Same Conversation
Generate five teaching approaches for a physics concept.
In the same conversation, immediately ask: "Now give me five metrics to evaluate these approaches."
Method B — Separate Conversation
Generate five teaching approaches for the same physics concept in Conversation A.
In a brand-new Conversation B, with no mention of the specific approaches, ask for five metrics for evaluating physics teaching approaches in general.
What to Compare
Do Method A's metrics seem to closely track features already present in the five generated approaches (suggesting bias)?
Do Method B's metrics feel more general and independently reasoned, potentially surfacing a dimension none of the five approaches actually addressed well?
Key Takeaway

If Method A's metrics suspiciously "fit" the existing options too neatly, that's evidence of the bias the lecture warns about — a sign to default to Method B (separate conversations) for genuinely important evaluations.

Case Study 4: Reformat and Combine Options With Metrics

Goal: Reproduce the full lecture workflow — generate, reformat, then evaluate — for a physics teaching decision.

Steps
Generate five different ways to teach Newton's Third Law (Conversation A).
Generate five evaluation metrics for physics teaching approaches, independently (Conversation B).
Ask the AI to reformat all five teaching approaches into a consistent structure (e.g., "Approach Name → Description → Time Required → Materials Needed").
In a new prompt, combine the reformatted approaches and the metrics: "Evaluate these five teaching approaches using these five metrics."
What to Evaluate
Does the final structured evaluation make it easier to compare approaches side by side than reading the five original approaches would have been?
Did any new teaching approach get suggested during this process that wasn't part of the original five (mirroring the lecture's "foil packet cake" surprise)?
Key Takeaway

The combination of independently generated metrics and consistently formatted options produces a genuinely comparable, criteria-by-criteria evaluation — turning a subjective "which one do I like best" decision into a structured comparison.

Case Study 5: Extend Into a Quadrant Chart

Goal: Test the lecture's suggestion of extending the evaluation into a visual comparison format.

Prompt Used

"Put this evaluation into a quadrant chart, with 'class time required' on one axis and 'expected student engagement' on the other axis."

What to Evaluate
Does the resulting chart provide a genuinely useful at-a-glance comparison for choosing a teaching approach?
Would this be useful for a real scenario, like justifying a lesson-design choice in a department meeting?
Key Takeaway

Extending a text-based evaluation into a visual quadrant chart makes trade-offs immediately visible, which is especially useful when communicating a teaching decision to colleagues or administrators who need a quick, clear comparison rather than a detailed written evaluation.

Key Takeaway From This Case Study

Separating metric generation from solution generation — and doing so in an independent conversation — produces evaluation criteria that are more genuinely objective and less likely to simply flatter whatever options already exist. For physics teaching decisions specifically, this workflow (generate options → generate independent metrics → reformat → evaluate → optionally visualize) turns intuitive teaching choices into structured, defensible decisions, while still leaving room for the teacher's own judgment about what ultimately fits their specific students.
