Prompt Engineering Case Study
Case Study 1: Compare Independent vs. Non-Independent Generation

Goal: Directly test the lesson's claim that a single prompt requesting multiple outputs produces less diverse results than independent generations.

Method A — Single Prompt, Multiple Outputs

"Give me three different explanations of why objects in orbit are in continuous free fall."

Method B — Independent Conversations

Submit the identical prompt below three separate times, each in a brand-new conversation:

"Explain why objects in orbit are in continuous free fall."

What to Compare
Read all three outputs from Method A side by side — are they meaningfully different in structure and analogy, or mostly reworded versions of the same explanation?
Read all three outputs from Method B side by side — do they show more variation in structure, analogy choice, or framing than Method A?
Expected Observation

Method B (independent conversations) likely produces more genuinely distinct explanations, since each one was generated without visibility into the others, while Method A's three outputs may share more structural similarity since the model could see all three as it generated them.

Case Study 2: Test the Effect of Specifying the Level of Abstraction

Goal: Directly compare word-level variation against approach-level variation for the same physics topic.

Prompt A — Word-Level Variation (Unspecified)

"Give me three explanations of Newton's Third Law."

Prompt B — Approach-Level Variation (Explicit)

"Consider fundamentally different approaches to explaining Newton's Third Law to a 9th grade class — for example, an everyday-object analogy approach, a hands-on demonstration approach, and a common-misconception-correction approach. Write an explanation using each approach."

What to Evaluate
Do Prompt A's three explanations differ mainly in wording, or do they actually use different structures/analogies?
Do Prompt B's three explanations serve genuinely different teaching purposes (e.g., one better for a visual learner, one better for correcting a specific misconception)?
Key Takeaway

Explicitly naming the level of abstraction (approach-level, in Prompt B) is expected to produce options with real pedagogical differences, while leaving variation unspecified (Prompt A) risks producing options that are only trivially different.

Case Study 3: Apply the "Unit of Variation" Concept to a Physics Lesson

Goal: Test whether explicitly naming a specific unit of variation (rather than "different approaches" in general) produces a more targeted, usable set of options.

Prompt Used

"Give me four ways to teach the concept of electric circuits, each targeting a different common student misconception: (1) confusing current with voltage, (2) believing current is 'used up' as it flows through a circuit, (3) believing all circuit elements in series carry different current, (4) confusing series and parallel circuit behavior. For each, describe the teaching approach and how it directly addresses that specific misconception."

What to Evaluate
Does defining the unit of variation as "misconception targeted" (rather than a vague "different approaches") produce four outputs that are each clearly usable for a specific classroom moment?
Is this more directly actionable for lesson planning than a generic "give me four ways to teach circuits" prompt would have been?
Key Takeaway

Naming a specific, meaningful unit of variation (misconception targeted, in this case) transforms the output from a generic set of teaching ideas into four purpose-built tools, each ready to deploy at the exact moment a specific misconception appears in class.

Case Study 4: Test Using Multiple AI Tools for a High-Stakes Curriculum Decision

Goal: Apply the lesson's suggestion of using different large language model tools as an additional source of independent perspectives, for a genuinely important decision.

Method
Ask the same question in two or more separate AI tools (e.g., Claude and another available assistant): "What is the best sequence of topics for introducing quantum mechanics concepts to a class that has only covered classical mechanics so far, and why?"
Compare the suggested sequences and reasoning across tools.
What to Evaluate
Do the different tools suggest meaningfully different sequencing strategies, or largely the same one?
Does having a second, independent tool's perspective change my confidence in the sequencing approach I would have chosen on my own?
Key Takeaway

For decisions with real, lasting classroom impact — like how to structure the introduction to a genuinely difficult new unit — the extra time cost of checking a second independent tool is small relative to the value of confirming (or challenging) a major pedagogical choice before committing a unit's worth of class time to it.

Key Takeaway From This Case Study

Generating "multiple options" is not a single technique — it's a family of related techniques with real trade-offs between independence and convenience, and real differences depending on what level of abstraction the variation is specified at. For physics teaching specifically, the biggest practical lever is moving variation up from the word level to the approach, framework, or misconception level — this is what turns a generic "give me three explanations" into a genuinely useful, purpose-built set of teaching tools.
