Reflection
What Changed My Perspective

This lesson changed how I think about what "asking for options" actually requires to work well.

Before this lesson, I assumed that asking for "three different emails" or "five different approaches" was, by itself, enough to get genuine diversity. I now understand that the method matters — a single prompt requesting multiple outputs at once can produce options that are only superficially different, because the model can see all of them and may keep them following a similar trajectory. Real diversity often requires independent generations, or explicitly naming the level at which things should vary.

Key Insight

One of the most valuable lessons I learned is the idea of "levels of abstraction" for variation.

Asking for different word choices produces weak diversity. Asking for fundamentally different approaches — informal vs. professional, brief vs. personal — produces genuinely different outputs built on different underlying strategies. This reframes how I should be writing "give me options" prompts: instead of just asking for more of something, I should be specifying exactly what dimension I want to vary, and at what level.

Relevance to Physics Education

As someone preparing for a career as a Physics AI Subject Matter Expert, this lesson gives me a much sharper tool for generating genuinely different lesson explanations, not just superficially reworded ones.

If I ask for three explanations of a physics concept without specifying a level of variation, I might get three versions that use nearly the same structure with different word choices — not very useful. But if I specify "give me three explanations using fundamentally different approaches — one analogy-driven, one derivation-driven, one misconception-driven" — I get genuinely distinct teaching tools I can choose between or combine, matched to different students' needs.

Questions for Further Exploration

This lesson also raised a few questions I want to carry into future modules:

In practice, how do I know when a single-prompt "give me three" request is producing options that are too similar, versus genuinely diverse enough to be useful?
Is there real added value in testing the same physics explanation prompt across different AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini), or is that mostly useful for very high-stakes decisions?
How do I build a personal library of "levels of variation" I commonly use for physics teaching (e.g., analogy-based, derivation-based, misconception-based, real-world-application-based) so I don't have to invent this from scratch each time?
Does explicitly naming a technique or framework (like the lecture's marketing framework example) have a physics-teaching equivalent — like naming specific pedagogical frameworks (inquiry-based, direct instruction, flipped classroom) and asking the AI to apply each one?
Action Plan

Based on this lesson, I will:

Stop defaulting to vague "give me three options" prompts and start explicitly naming the dimension of variation I want (approach, technique, tone, pedagogical framework).
Use independent conversations (rather than a single multi-output prompt) when I actually need maximum diversity for an important decision, like designing a new unit.
Build a small personal list of "variation dimensions" specific to physics teaching — analogy type, pedagogical framework, difficulty level — to reuse across future prompts.
Occasionally test the same physics prompt across more than one AI tool when the decision is important enough to warrant the extra effort.
Final Reflection

This lesson sharpened a technique I'd already started using:

Asking for multiple options is only as useful as the diversity of those options — and real diversity comes from deliberately choosing both the generation method (independent vs. single-prompt) and the level of abstraction at which things vary.

Combined with the previous lesson's "ask for options" pattern, I now have a much more precise toolkit: not just generate multiple, but generate multiple that differ in a specific, meaningful way. For physics teaching, this means my future option-generating prompts will name the actual dimension I want varied — analogy, framework, or approach — rather than leaving diversity to chance.
