Module 2, Lesson 3 Notes
Methods for Generating Diverse Options: Independence, Abstraction Level, and Sources of Variation
Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, I should be able to:

Identify multiple distinct methods for generating multiple options with generative AI (edit-and-regenerate, new conversation, single-prompt multiple-generation, different tools/models).
Explain the trade-off between generating options within one conversation versus across independent conversations.
Understand the concept of "levels of abstraction" when requesting variation — word-level, approach-level, technique-level, or framework-level.
Recognize why explicitly defining the "unit of variation" produces more genuinely diverse and useful options.
Apply these different methods deliberately, choosing the right one based on how independent or how deep the variation needs to be.
Key Concepts
1. Multiple Methods Exist for Generating Options

This lesson expands on the "ask for options" pattern from Lesson 2 by breaking down several distinct methods for actually producing those multiple options, each with different trade-offs:

Edit-and-regenerate within one conversation — make a small tweak to the prompt (even a minor one, like adding a space) and regenerate.
New, independent conversation with the same or reworded prompt — start fresh each time.
Single prompt requesting multiple outputs at once (e.g., "give me three different emails...") — one generation event, multiple outputs.
Different large language model tools entirely (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) — collect independent perspectives across different underlying models.
2. The Core Trade-Off: Independence vs. Convenience

The central technical distinction in this lesson is between independent generations and non-independent (context-aware) generations:

Independent generations (separate conversations, or even separate tools): each output is generated without seeing any of the others. This tends to produce more genuinely diverse, unique options because nothing is anchoring one output to resemble another.
Non-independent generations (a single prompt asking for multiple outputs at once, like "give me three emails"): the model can see all the options it's generating within that same response. This is more convenient and faster, but risks the options being less distinct from each other, since they may follow the same underlying trajectory or pattern.

Practical implication: If maximum diversity matters most, use independent conversations (or independent tools). If speed and convenience matter more, a single "give me three options" prompt is simpler and often good enough.

3. Multiple Ways to Introduce Independence

Beyond just starting a new conversation, this lesson identifies several dimensions along which independent generations can vary:

Varying the prompt wording while keeping the same underlying request.
Reusing the exact same prompt in a new conversation (relying on the model's inherent variability).
Varying the underlying model/tool being used (ChatGPT vs. Claude vs. Gemini, etc.).
4. The Critical Idea: Levels of Abstraction for Variation

This is the most conceptually important idea in the lesson. Instead of only varying content at the word level (slightly different phrasing of the same basic email), you can instruct the model to vary at a higher level of abstraction:

Word level: Same basic content, different phrasing (weakest form of diversity).
Approach level: "Consider fundamentally different approaches to announcing this course via email" (e.g., informal vs. professional, personal and enthusiastic vs. brief and direct) — this produces genuinely different emails built on different underlying strategies, not just different wording of the same strategy.
Technique/framework level: If you have several named techniques or frameworks (e.g., five different marketing frameworks), you can explicitly instruct the model to apply each one and generate results based on that specific technique.

Why this matters: Simply asking for "three different emails" without specifying the level of variation often produces outputs that differ only superficially. Explicitly naming the dimension of variation (approach, technique, tone, framework) produces outputs that are meaningfully, structurally different from each other.

5. Defining the "Unit of Variation"

The lesson introduces the idea of asking: what is the fundamental unit of variation across the options being generated? Is it:

The specific words/phrasing used?
The overall approach or strategy?
The technique or framework applied?
The tone?
The underlying model generating it?

Being deliberate about this — rather than leaving it undefined — is what separates a genuinely diverse set of options from a set of outputs that are only trivially different from each other.

6. The Overall Workflow This Enables

The lesson frames the process as: explore quickly and broadly (since generation is fast and low-cost), collect a range of perspectives and potential solutions, and then bring human judgment back in to evaluate them — including the valid possibility that the original idea you started with turns out to still be the best one after due diligence.

Important Terminology
Independent Generation

An AI output produced without visibility into any other generated output for the same task (e.g., from a separate conversation or a different model), which tends to produce more genuinely diverse results.

Non-Independent (Context-Aware) Generation

Multiple AI outputs produced within the same response or conversation, where the model can see and is influenced by the other outputs it is simultaneously generating.

Level of Abstraction (for Variation)

The conceptual level at which requested variation occurs — ranging from word-level phrasing differences, up through approach-level strategy differences, up to technique- or framework-level differences.

Unit of Variation

The specific dimension deliberately chosen to differ across a set of generated options (e.g., tone, approach, technique, underlying model), as opposed to leaving variation undefined and left to chance.

Main Ideas
There are multiple distinct methods for generating diverse options: editing and regenerating, starting new conversations, requesting multiple outputs in one prompt, and using entirely different AI tools.
Independent generations (separate conversations or tools) tend to produce more genuinely diverse results than a single prompt requesting multiple outputs at once, because the model can't see and be influenced by its own other options in the independent case.
The most powerful lever for real diversity isn't which method you use to generate multiple outputs — it's specifying the level of abstraction at which they should vary (word choice vs. approach vs. technique/framework).
Explicitly defining the "unit of variation" produces meaningfully different options rather than superficially different ones.
The overall goal remains the same as previous lessons: explore broadly and quickly, then apply human judgment to evaluate and select — including possibly confirming that the original approach was already the best one.
Professional Insights

This lesson adds important nuance to the "ask for options" technique introduced in the prior lesson: not all methods of generating multiple options are equally effective, and the choice of method should match the goal.

For an AI Subject Matter Expert, this has direct implications:

When training others to generate diverse perspectives, it's important to teach not just "ask for more than one," but how to ask in a way that produces genuine diversity — specifying the level of variation rather than leaving it to chance.
Recognizing the trade-off between independent and non-independent generation is a diagnostic skill: if generated options all look similar despite asking for "three different X," the fix is often to either use independent conversations or explicitly name the dimension of variation.
This level-of-abstraction thinking is broadly transferable — it applies to any domain requiring diverse option generation, not just writing tasks.
Common Mistakes

Common mistakes related to this lesson's concept include:

Assuming a single prompt requesting multiple outputs ("give me three emails") will produce equally diverse results as independent generations.
Only varying content at the word/phrasing level, then being surprised when the resulting options feel repetitive or interchangeable.
Not explicitly specifying what should vary between the requested options (tone? approach? framework?), leaving the model to decide arbitrarily.
Sticking to only one generation method (e.g., always using a single prompt for multiple outputs) rather than choosing the method that fits the actual goal (speed vs. maximum diversity).
Practical Takeaways

After completing this lesson, I can:

Choose between independent generation (new conversations, different tools) and single-prompt multi-generation based on whether maximum diversity or convenience matters more for a given task.
Explicitly specify the level of abstraction at which I want generated options to vary — word choice, approach, technique, or framework.
Define a clear "unit of variation" before requesting multiple options, rather than leaving diversity to chance.
Use multiple AI tools/models as an additional source of independent perspectives when maximum diversity is valuable.
Apply this deliberate approach to generating diverse options in my own physics teaching materials and problem-solving.
