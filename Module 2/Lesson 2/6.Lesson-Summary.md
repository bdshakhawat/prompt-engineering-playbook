# Lesson Summary

## Module 2, Lesson 2: The "Ask for Options" Pattern

**One-Sentence Summary:** Never ask generative AI for just one solution to a problem — ask for multiple options and an explicit comparison of their trade-offs, since this simple, nearly-free technique widens perspective, surfaces overlooked considerations, and produces genuinely better decisions on problems that matter.

### The Core Technique
The "ask for options" pattern has two required parts:
1. Request a specific number of distinct solutions (e.g., "give me five ways to...").
2. Explicitly instruct the model to compare and contrast them.

Requesting multiple options alone isn't enough — the comparison step is where trade-offs surface and real thinking gets triggered.

### The Demonstrations
- **Webinar registration problem:** Generated five genuinely different solution approaches, including a no-code platform option the instructor likely wouldn't have considered independently.
- **Camping cake-baking problem:** Generated three resource-constrained cooking methods, with a comparison surfacing a specific risk (heat control) that wasn't obvious from the method descriptions alone.

### The ROI Argument
Consistent with the previous lesson, the value of this technique is tied to the importance of the problem and the quality of the resulting decision — not to time saved. The instructor frames this as potentially transformative: either leading to a meaningfully better decision, or guarding against never having considered a superior option.

### The Necessary Caution
A generated comparison's stated conclusion (e.g., "Method 3 has the best outcome") should be evaluated, not accepted automatically — the model doesn't know constraints or context that weren't explicitly provided.

### Physics Teaching Connection
This pattern extends naturally to classroom and curriculum decisions — lesson design, lab design with limited equipment, and assessment design. Specifying the exact comparison criteria that matter (e.g., measurement accuracy, class time, misconception-targeting) produces a far more directly usable comparison than a generic pros/cons request, and any "best option" verdict should still be checked against real classroom constraints the AI wasn't given.
