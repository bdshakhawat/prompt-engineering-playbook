Physics AI Application
Purpose of This File

This file translates this lesson's core technique — the "ask for options" pattern (request multiple solutions plus a comparison of trade-offs) — into physics teaching and curriculum-planning contexts. The goal is to show how this pattern supports real pedagogical decisions, not just writing tasks.

1. The "Ask for Options" Pattern — Physics Version
Original Lecture Example

"Give me five ways to solve [a webinar registration problem] and compare and contrast them" surfaced options the instructor wouldn't have considered independently, including a no-code platform approach.

Physics Classroom Equivalent

Prompt:

"Give me five ways to teach the concept of momentum conservation to a 10th grade class with only basic lab equipment (marbles, ramps, a stopwatch), and compare and contrast them by required class time, likely student engagement, and how directly each addresses the common 'momentum vs. force' misconception."

Likely result: Several genuinely different approaches — a hands-on collision lab, a video-based demonstration with guided notes, a simulation-based activity, a small-group problem-solving station rotation, and a Socratic-style discussion built around a real-world scenario — each with trade-offs across the three comparison criteria specified.

Why this matters for teaching: Left to my own instinct, I'd likely default to whatever method I've used most often. Asking for five options and an explicit comparison surfaces alternatives I might not have considered, the same way the instructor discovered the no-code platform option for their registration system problem.

2. Applying This to a Lab-Design Problem
Physics Classroom Equivalent to the Camping Cake Example

The instructor's camping example (bake a cake with tinfoil, a skillet, and fire) is a resource-constrained problem-solving task — a good parallel to a common physics teaching situation: designing a lab with limited equipment.

Prompt:

"I'm teaching projectile motion and only have access to a stopwatch, meter sticks, and a small foam launcher — no motion sensors or photogates. Give me three ways to have students collect meaningful projectile motion data with this equipment, and compare and contrast them by measurement accuracy, setup time, and risk of student error."

Value of the comparison step: Just as the instructor's comparison surfaced a heat-control risk in the skillet-baking method, a physics-specific comparison here would surface risks like timing inconsistency (human reaction time affecting stopwatch measurements) or measurement error from launch angle inconsistency — considerations that directly affect whether the lab produces usable data.

3. Applying This to Assessment and Grading Decisions
Practical Application

Beyond lesson and lab design, the "ask for options" pattern can support decisions about how to assess student understanding.

Prompt:

"Give me four different ways to assess whether students understand the difference between speed and velocity, ranging from traditional written questions to more creative formats, and compare and contrast them by how well each distinguishes surface-level memorization from genuine conceptual understanding."

Why the comparison matters here specifically: A written multiple-choice question might be fast to grade but easy to answer through memorization; a scenario-based question might better reveal genuine understanding but take longer to grade. Requesting an explicit comparison on this specific criterion (not just general "pros and cons") makes the trade-off directly relevant to the actual decision I need to make.

4. The Same Caution Applies: Don't Accept the "Best" Verdict Uncritically

Just as the instructor questioned whether they actually agreed that "Method 3 has the best outcome" in the cake example, any generated comparison's conclusion about a teaching method needs the same scrutiny.

Physics-specific example: If a generated comparison claims a simulation-based activity is superior to a hands-on lab for teaching momentum, that conclusion should be evaluated against what I actually know about my specific students — for instance, whether they engage more with tactile, hands-on activities than with a screen-based simulation. The AI can surface options and trade-offs, but it doesn't know my classroom the way I do.

5. Practical Application: My Own "Ask for Options" Workflow for Physics Teaching
Use this pattern for any non-trivial teaching decision — lesson design, lab design, assessment design — not just for writing tasks.
Specify the comparison criteria that actually matter for the decision (e.g., equipment needs, class time, misconception-targeting, measurement accuracy) rather than accepting a generic pros/cons list.
Treat the generated comparison as a decision-support framework, not a final answer — evaluate any stated "best" option against what I know about my specific students and constraints.
Reserve extra scrutiny for safety-related decisions (like lab procedures), where blindly accepting a generated recommendation carries more risk than in a low-stakes decision.
Combine with iterative refinement (Lesson 3) by narrowing to the strongest option from a generated set, then refining that specific option further through follow-up conversation.
