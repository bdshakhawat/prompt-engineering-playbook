Reflection
What Changed My Perspective

This lesson took something I learned in the previous lesson and showed me it applies far beyond writing tasks.

Before this lesson, I understood "exploiting generation" mainly in the context of comparing different drafts of the same email or explanation. I now see that the exact same principle — generate multiple, then compare — applies to actual problem-solving: choosing a system, a method, a strategy, even something as practical as how to bake a cake with limited tools. The "ask for options" pattern turns AI into a genuine decision-support tool, not just a writing assistant.

Key Insight

One of the most valuable lessons I learned is that the comparison step, not the generation step, is where the real value comes from.

Simply asking for five solutions isn't enough on its own — it's the explicit instruction to compare and contrast them that forces trade-offs to the surface. In the camping cake example, generating three methods was useful, but seeing them compared on heat control, ease of use, and likely outcome is what actually taught something new — like the fact that skillet baking on coals risks burning the bottom. That insight came from the comparison, not from the list itself.

Relevance to Physics Education

As someone preparing for a career as a Physics AI Subject Matter Expert, I can see immediate use for this pattern beyond writing lesson materials — specifically for solving classroom and curriculum problems.

If I'm trying to figure out how to teach a difficult concept to a class with mixed ability levels, or how to structure a lab with limited equipment, I don't have to rely on my own first instinct. I can ask for five different approaches and a comparison of their trade-offs — pacing risk, equipment needs, likely student engagement — the same way the instructor compared registration system options. This reframes lesson planning itself as a problem I can systematically explore, not just something I generate once and commit to.

Questions for Further Exploration

This lesson also raised a few questions I want to carry into future modules:

How do I decide the right number of options to request — is five always better than three, or does that depend on the complexity of the problem?
How should I evaluate a generated comparison's stated conclusion (e.g., "Method 3 has the best outcome") when I don't have direct experience to check it against, especially outside my subject-matter expertise?
Can this pattern be combined with iterative refinement from Lesson 3 — for example, generating five options, narrowing to one, and then refining that one further through follow-up conversation?
Are there problem types in physics teaching (like safety-critical lab procedures) where blindly trusting a generated "best option" would be risky, even with a comparison provided?
Action Plan

Based on this lesson, I will:

Default to the "ask for options" pattern for any non-trivial classroom or curriculum decision, not just writing tasks — explicitly requesting a number of options and a comparison.
Treat generated comparisons as a framework for my own evaluation, not as a final verdict, especially for anything safety-related.
Practice specifying the comparison criteria that matter most for a given decision (e.g., "compare by time required and equipment needed" for a lab activity).
Use this pattern specifically for decisions I'd otherwise default to my first instinct on, since that's where the value of surfacing unconsidered options is highest.
