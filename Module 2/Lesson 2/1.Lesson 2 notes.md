# Key Concepts

## 1. From Writing Tasks to Problem-Solving Tasks

Earlier in the course, "exploiting generation" was applied to writing tasks (like an email). This lesson extends the same underlying principle to a much broader and higher-stakes category: **solving problems in general** — technical, logistical, or even everyday practical problems.

The instructor frames this as one of the most fundamentally important uses of prompt engineering: using generative AI to explore multiple ways of solving a problem, not just to produce a single answer.

## 2. The "Ask for Options" Pattern

The core technique introduced in this lesson is simple and reusable:

> Never ask for one of something — ask for more than one, and ask the model to compare and contrast them.

Example prompt structure used in the lecture:
> "Give me five ways to solve [problem] and compare and contrast them."

This pattern has two required components:
1. A specific number of distinct solutions requested (e.g., three, five).
2. An explicit instruction to compare and contrast the options (not just list them).

## 3. Why "Compare and Contrast" Matters as Much as "Give Me Multiple"

Simply generating multiple solutions is not enough on its own — the instructor emphasizes that requesting a **comparison of pros and cons** is where real thinking is triggered. Comparing solutions:

- Surfaces trade-offs the person might not have considered (e.g., heat control issues with skillet baking).
- Provides a framework for evaluating options, not just a list to choose from arbitrarily.
- Forces active engagement with *why* one option might be better than another, rather than passive selection.

## 4. Real-World Demonstrations from the Lecture

### Example 1: Webinar Registration System
Prompt: "Give me five ways to solve [a webinar registration and personalized follow-up problem] and compare and contrast them."

Result: Five genuinely different approaches (Google Forms/Sheets, Eventbrite, custom web app, CRM system like HubSpot, no-code platform like Airtable/Zapier), each with pros and cons. The instructor notes that one option (the no-code platform approach) was something they likely would not have considered without generating multiple perspectives.

### Example 2: Camping Cake-Baking Problem
Prompt: "Give me three ways to bake a cake with only tinfoil, a skillet, and fire, and compare and contrast the difficulty of getting the cake to turn out."

Result: Three distinct methods (tinfoil oven, skillet baking, dutch oven substitute), each with a full comparison across heat control, outcome, and ease of use. The instructor highlights that this level of accessible, on-demand problem-solving support — across an essentially unlimited range of arbitrary problems — was not realistically possible even with significant computing resources and expertise just a few years earlier.

## 5. The Real ROI Is Tied to Decision Importance, Not Time Saved

The instructor reiterates and sharpens the ROI argument from the previous lesson: the value of using the "ask for options" pattern is a function of **how important the problem is** and **how different (and better) the resulting decision is**, not how many minutes were saved.

This value is described as difficult to measure using standard metrics, but potentially transformative — either by leading to a meaningfully better decision, or by guarding against never having considered an option that turned out to be superior.

## 6. This Technique Enriches Thinking, Not Replaces It

Even though this pattern can sometimes lead to a faster answer (especially when the person has no prior idea how to approach a problem), the instructor is clear that speed is not the primary goal or benefit. The main benefit is that exploring multiple approaches and their trade-offs **requires more thinking**, not less — it widens perspective and surfaces considerations (like heat control risk in the cake example) the person may not have identified independently.

# Important Terminology

## Ask for Options Pattern

A reusable prompt engineering technique in which a person requests multiple distinct solutions to a problem (a specified number) along with an explicit comparison of their pros and cons, rather than requesting a single solution.

## Compare and Contrast Instruction

An explicit prompt component that asks the model not just to list multiple options, but to evaluate them against each other — surfacing trade-offs, risks, and relative strengths.

## Decision-Importance-Based ROI

A framing of return on investment in which the value of using generative AI is measured by the significance of the problem and the quality of the resulting decision, rather than by time saved.

---

# Main Ideas

- The "ask for options" pattern — request multiple solutions and a comparison of their pros and cons — is one of the simplest and highest-leverage prompt engineering techniques available.
- Requesting a comparison, not just multiple options, is what actually triggers deeper thinking and decision-making.
- This pattern applies broadly, from technical/business problems (a registration system) to everyday practical problems (baking a cake with limited tools).
- The value of this technique is tied to the importance of the problem being solved, not to the time saved generating the response.
- Exploring multiple solutions and their trade-offs enriches thinking and widens perspective rather than replacing critical thinking with a shortcut.

# Professional Insights

This lesson positions prompt engineering as fundamentally about problem-solving and decision support, not just content generation. For an AI Subject Matter Expert, the "ask for options" pattern has direct professional value:

- It provides a lightweight, repeatable way to surface options a person might not have considered on their own, functioning as a decision-support tool rather than an answer-generation tool.
- It builds in a comparison framework (pros/cons, trade-offs) automatically, which supports more defensible, better-reasoned decisions.
- It scales to essentially any domain or problem type, making it broadly applicable across professional contexts, including education and curriculum planning.

---

# Common Mistakes

Common mistakes related to this lesson's concept include:

- Asking for a single solution to a problem instead of requesting multiple options by default.
- Requesting multiple options without also asking for a comparison, missing the step that actually drives deeper thinking.
- Accepting a generated comparison's conclusion (e.g., "Method 3 has the best outcome") without independently evaluating whether that conclusion is actually correct for the specific situation.
- Measuring the value of this technique by speed rather than by the quality or importance of the resulting decision.
