# Physics AI Best Practices

> A practical framework for designing, using, evaluating, and improving AI systems for Physics-related tasks.

**Status:** Active Development  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Domain:** Physics + Generative AI + Prompt Engineering + AI Evaluation  
**Author:** Muhammad Shakhawat Hossain

---

## 1. Purpose

Physics AI requires more than producing fluent answers.

A useful Physics AI system must be:

- Scientifically accurate
- Conceptually clear
- Grounded in appropriate evidence
- Appropriate for the target learner or task
- Consistent with instructions
- Transparent about uncertainty
- Resistant to hallucination
- Evaluatable using explicit criteria

The central principle of this knowledge base is:

> **Use AI to augment Physics expertise, not to replace Physics expertise.**

The human SME remains responsible for determining whether an AI-generated Physics response is scientifically and pedagogically acceptable.

# 2. The Physics AI Quality Framework

Every Physics AI output should be evaluated across several dimensions.

```text
                    PHYSICS AI QUALITY
                           |
        +------------------+------------------+
        |                  |                  |
   SCIENTIFIC          EDUCATIONAL          AI
   ACCURACY             QUALITY           RELIABILITY
        |                  |                  |
   Correct laws       Clear explanation   Follows prompt
   Correct equations  Correct level       Avoids hallucination
   Correct units      Useful examples     Uses context
   Correct reasoning  Misconception aware States uncertainty

   ============================================================================================================================================

   3. Scientific Accuracy Comes First

For Physics-related tasks, scientific accuracy is the primary requirement.

Check:

Physical laws
Definitions
Equations
Units
Signs
Directions
Mathematical relationships
Assumptions
Boundary conditions
Numerical calculations
Physical interpretation
Example

If an AI states:

"Momentum is a force that keeps an object moving."

This may sound plausible to a beginner but is conceptually incorrect.

A Physics SME should distinguish:

Momentum as a physical quantity
Force as an interaction that changes momentum

The goal is not merely to detect incorrect wording but to identify the underlying conceptual error.

4. Separate Conceptual Accuracy from Mathematical Accuracy

Physics answers can fail in different ways.

An AI response may have:

Conceptual error

The underlying Physics principle is wrong.

Mathematical error

The correct principle is selected, but the calculation is wrong.

Unit error

The numerical result may be correct but the unit is incorrect or missing.

Sign / Direction error

The magnitude may be correct but the direction or sign is wrong.

Interpretation error

The calculation is correct but the physical meaning of the result is misunderstood.

Instruction-following error

The Physics may be correct but the response does not satisfy the requested task.

These errors should be evaluated separately.

5. Do Not Trust Fluent AI Output Automatically

Generative AI can produce convincing explanations even when the content is incorrect.

Therefore, do not use:

Fluency
Confidence
Length
Formatting
Technical vocabulary

as substitutes for verification.

Instead ask:

Is the claim scientifically correct?
What evidence supports it?
Are the equations appropriate?
Are the assumptions valid?
Are the units consistent?
Does the conclusion follow from the reasoning?
6. Ground the Model When Accuracy Matters

When reliable source information is available, provide it to the model.

Useful sources may include:

Course materials
Textbooks
Laboratory instructions
Research papers
Institutional documentation
Curated knowledge bases
Verified datasets
Official technical documentation

A grounded workflow can be represented as:

Reliable Source
      ↓
Relevant Information
      ↓
Prompt / Context
      ↓
AI Response
      ↓
SME Verification
      ↓
Approved Output

The model should not be encouraged to fill missing information with plausible guesses.

7. Handle Information Gaps Explicitly

A strong Physics AI workflow should distinguish between:

Known information
Inferred information
Assumed information
Unknown information

Use explicit instructions such as:

Do not invent missing information.

If the provided information is insufficient:
1. Identify what is missing.
2. Explain why it is required.
3. State what can still be concluded.

This is especially important for:

Numerical problems
Laboratory analysis
Scientific explanations
Research summaries
AI evaluation
Training data generation
8. Manage Hallucination Risk

Hallucination occurs when an AI system produces unsupported or fabricated information.

Common Physics hallucinations may include:

Invented equations
Incorrect constants
Fabricated experimental results
Incorrect historical claims
Nonexistent scientific sources
Unsupported interpretations
Incorrect numerical values
Confidently stated assumptions
Hallucination Prevention Workflow
Define Source
     ↓
Provide Relevant Context
     ↓
Restrict Unsupported Claims
     ↓
Require Uncertainty Disclosure
     ↓
Verify Important Claims

Use prompts that explicitly say:

"Do not invent information that is not supported by the provided material."

9. Use Appropriate Prompt Structure

A Physics prompt should provide enough information for the task.

A useful structure is:

ROLE
↓
TASK
↓
PHYSICS CONTEXT
↓
INPUT
↓
CONSTRAINTS
↓
OUTPUT FORMAT
↓
EVALUATION CRITERIA

Not every prompt requires every component.

Avoid adding instructions merely to make a prompt longer.

The objective is clarity and control, not prompt length.

10. Use Domain-Specific Roles Carefully

A role such as:

"Act as a Physics Subject Matter Expert"

can establish the intended perspective.

However, a role instruction does not guarantee factual accuracy.

Therefore:

Role
+
Relevant Context
+
Clear Task
+
Constraints
+
Verification

is stronger than role prompting alone.

11. Use Examples When Behavior Needs to Be Demonstrated

Few-shot examples are useful when the desired behavior is difficult to describe completely with instructions.

For example:

Input:
[Physics question]

Output:
[Desired style of answer]

Input:
[Another Physics question]

Output:
[Desired style of answer]

Input:
[New Physics question]

Output:

Good examples should be:

Correct
Representative
Consistent
Relevant
Diverse enough to avoid overfitting to one case

Never use an incorrect Physics example merely because its writing style is good.

12. Design Prompts for the Target Audience

The same Physics concept may require very different explanations.

Consider:

Age
Grade level
Prior knowledge
Mathematical background
Language proficiency
Learning objective
Misconceptions

For example:

Concept
   ↓
Target Learner
   ↓
Required Depth
   ↓
Appropriate Explanation

Avoid using university-level mathematical language when explaining a concept to a beginner unless that complexity is necessary.

13. Explain Physical Meaning Before Mathematical Manipulation

When appropriate, Physics explanations should connect:

Physical Situation
        ↓
Concept
        ↓
Physical Meaning
        ↓
Mathematical Relationship
        ↓
Calculation
        ↓
Physical Interpretation

Equations should not become isolated symbolic manipulation.

For example, when presenting an equation, explain:

What the equation represents
What each variable means
How the quantities relate
What happens when a variable changes
Under what conditions the relationship applies
14. Treat Units as Part of the Reasoning

Units are not merely formatting.

They can help identify:

Incorrect equations
Incorrect substitutions
Calculation mistakes
Incorrect final answers
Conceptual misunderstandings

Whenever appropriate, prompts should require:

Given quantities
↓
Equation
↓
Substitution
↓
Unit handling
↓
Final unit
↓
Physical interpretation
15. Encourage Sanity Checks

A numerical answer should be checked for physical plausibility.

Useful checks include:

Magnitude
Sign
Units
Limiting behavior
Order of magnitude
Direction
Consistency with the physical situation

A useful instruction is:

"After calculating the result, perform a brief physical reasonableness check."

16. Distinguish Assumptions from Facts

AI systems may silently introduce assumptions.

For example:

Given:
Object moves on a surface.

Possible assumption:
Surface is frictionless.

These are not equivalent.

The model should explicitly distinguish:

Given fact: Information provided by the problem.

Assumption: Information introduced to make the problem solvable.

Inference: A conclusion derived from available information.

Unknown: Information that cannot be determined.

This distinction is particularly important in scientific reasoning.

17. Use Structured Output for Evaluation Tasks

When evaluating multiple AI outputs, structured formats improve consistency.

For example:

Criterion	Score	Evidence	Improvement
Physics Accuracy			
Conceptual Clarity			
Instruction Following			
Audience Fit			
Hallucination Risk			

This is preferable to asking:

"Which answer is better?"

without defining what "better" means.

18. Define Evaluation Criteria Before Evaluating

An evaluation should establish what counts as a good response.

For example:

Evaluation Criteria
        |
        +-- Physics Accuracy
        |
        +-- Reasoning Quality
        |
        +-- Instruction Following
        |
        +-- Clarity
        |
        +-- Audience Appropriateness
        |
        +-- Completeness
        |
        +-- Hallucination Risk

The evaluator should then apply the same criteria consistently.

19. Use Evidence-Based Evaluation

Do not evaluate an AI response based only on overall impression.

Instead:

Claim
 ↓
Evidence
 ↓
Evaluation
 ↓
Decision

For each significant problem, identify:

What the AI said.
Why it is problematic.
What evidence supports the judgment.
What the correct or improved version should be.

This makes evaluation more reproducible.

20. Separate Error Detection from Error Correction

When evaluating AI output, first identify the problem before rewriting the response.

Stage 1 — Detection

"The response states X."

Stage 2 — Verification

"X is inconsistent with the supplied Physics principle."

Stage 3 — Correction

"A scientifically accurate version is Y."

This prevents the evaluator from silently replacing the original output without documenting what was wrong.

21. Preserve the Student's Reasoning During Evaluation

For student responses, do not immediately replace an incorrect answer with the correct answer.

Instead determine:

Student Response
      ↓
What does the student understand?
      ↓
Where does the reasoning diverge?
      ↓
What misconception explains the error?
      ↓
What intervention could help?

This is especially useful for:

Physics tutoring
Misconception analysis
Educational AI
Assessment feedback
22. Do Not Over-Correct Correct Answers

An AI evaluator should distinguish between:

Incorrect
Partially correct
Correct but incomplete
Correct with unclear explanation
Correct and well-supported

Not every imperfect response requires rejection.

Evaluation should be proportional to the task.

23. Handle Ambiguous Questions Carefully

If a Physics question is ambiguous:

Identify the ambiguity.
Explain why it matters.
State the interpretation being used.
Solve or analyze under that interpretation.
Mention alternative interpretations when relevant.

Do not silently choose an interpretation when the difference could materially affect the answer.

24. Use AI as a Collaborator, Not an Authority

A useful workflow is:

Human SME
    ↓
Defines Problem
    ↓
AI
    ↓
Generates / Analyzes
    ↓
Human SME
    ↓
Verifies
    ↓
Refines
    ↓
Final Output

The model should assist reasoning, not become the final scientific authority.

25. Keep Human Oversight Strongest in High-Risk Tasks

Human review is particularly important for:

Scientific claims
Research interpretation
Laboratory procedures
Safety instructions
High-stakes assessments
Curriculum decisions
Medical or engineering applications of Physics
Published educational content
AI training datasets

The greater the consequence of an error, the stronger the verification process should be.

26. Optimize for Reproducibility

A good AI workflow should produce reasonably consistent results when the same task is repeated.

Improve reproducibility through:

Clear instructions
Defined inputs
Explicit criteria
Structured outputs
Examples
Evaluation rubrics
Versioned prompts
Documented assumptions

Avoid relying entirely on vague instructions such as:

"Give me a good Physics explanation."

Instead define what "good" means.

27. Version-Control Important Prompts

Prompts should be treated as reusable artifacts.

When a prompt becomes important:

Prompt v1
   ↓
Test
   ↓
Identify Failure
   ↓
Prompt v2
   ↓
Test
   ↓
Compare
   ↓
Approved Version

Record:

Prompt version
Date
Purpose
Changes
Test cases
Known limitations

This is particularly useful for professional AI evaluation and training workflows.

28. Test with Edge Cases

A prompt that works on normal examples may fail on unusual inputs.

Useful test cases include:

Normal case

A standard Physics problem.

Ambiguous case

A problem with insufficiently specified information.

Misconception case

A student response containing a common conceptual error.

Boundary case

An input near the limits of the intended task.

Out-of-scope case

An input the prompt should refuse or identify as outside scope.

Testing should therefore include more than successful examples.

29. Build Evaluation Datasets Carefully

When creating Physics AI evaluation data:

Use scientifically verified answers.
Include realistic user inputs.
Include both easy and difficult cases.
Include common misconceptions.
Include edge cases.
Avoid ambiguous labels unless ambiguity is intentional.
Document the evaluation criteria.
Separate source data from generated data.
Review examples for accidental errors.

A small, high-quality evaluation set can be more useful than a large, noisy dataset.

30. Avoid Synthetic Data Contamination

When generating synthetic Physics examples:

Define the task.
Define quality criteria.
Generate examples.
Review the examples.
Remove incorrect or ambiguous examples.
Verify the Physics.
Label the examples clearly.
Store provenance where appropriate.

Never assume that synthetic data is automatically high quality because an AI generated it.

31. Protect Data Quality in AI Training

For Physics AI training or annotation workflows, inspect:

Input quality
Label consistency
Scientific correctness
Instruction consistency
Duplicate examples
Contradictory examples
Ambiguous examples
Missing information
Annotation errors

A useful principle is:

Bad training data can teach an AI system the wrong Physics very consistently.

32. Use Taxonomies Carefully

Taxonomies are useful for organizing:

Physics concepts
Misconceptions
Question types
Error types
Student responses
AI failure modes
Evaluation categories

A good taxonomy should be:

Evidence-based
Hierarchical
Understandable
Consistent
Useful for downstream analysis

Do not create categories merely to make a tree look comprehensive.

33. Prefer Specific Error Labels

Instead of:

"Student doesn't understand Physics."

Use a more specific classification when supported by evidence:

Mechanics
└── Momentum
    ├── Momentum vs Force
    ├── Conservation of Momentum
    └── Direction / Sign

Specific categories make subsequent teaching or evaluation interventions more actionable.

34. Avoid Over-Prompting

More instructions do not automatically produce better outputs.

Before adding another instruction, ask:

What failure does this instruction address?
Has that failure actually occurred?
Can the instruction be stated more clearly?
Does the new instruction conflict with another instruction?
Does it make the prompt unnecessarily complex?

Prompt engineering should be iterative and evidence-driven.

35. Prefer Testable Instructions

Weak:

"Give a really good answer."

Stronger:

"Identify the relevant Physics principle, explain its physical meaning, state the applicable equation, define the variables, and perform a reasonableness check."

The second instruction creates observable criteria that can be evaluated.

36. Avoid False Precision

AI should not present uncertain information with unnecessary confidence.

When evidence is insufficient, use language such as:

"The provided information does not establish..."
"This depends on..."
"An assumption is required..."
"This cannot be determined from the available information."

Uncertainty is preferable to fabricated certainty.

37. Maintain Scope Control

A Physics AI system should remain within the intended task.

If a prompt is designed to evaluate Newtonian mechanics, it should not unnecessarily expand into unrelated areas.

Use explicit scope boundaries when appropriate:

Scope:
[What the system should analyze]

Out of Scope:
[What it should not attempt]
38. Use Output Constraints Strategically

Useful constraints include:

Word limits
Required sections
Tables
Numbered steps
Specific scoring scales
JSON or structured formats
Required evidence
Required uncertainty statements

Constraints should support the task rather than create unnecessary formatting complexity.

39. Separate Generation from Evaluation

For important workflows, consider:

Stage 1: Generate
        ↓
Stage 2: Evaluate
        ↓
Stage 3: Revise
        ↓
Stage 4: Re-evaluate

This is often more reliable than asking the model to generate a response and declare it perfect in the same step.

40. Use Multiple Perspectives for Complex Tasks

For complex Physics AI tasks, different analytical lenses can reveal different problems.

Possible lenses:

Scientific Lens

Is the Physics correct?

Educational Lens

Is it understandable and appropriate for the learner?

Evaluation Lens

Can the response be scored consistently?

Reliability Lens

What could cause the AI to fail?

Practical Lens

Can the proposed workflow actually be implemented?

This supports more robust analysis than relying on a single perspective.

41. Recommended Physics AI Workflow

For a professional Physics AI task, use the following general workflow:

1. Define the task
        ↓
2. Define the target audience
        ↓
3. Gather reliable context
        ↓
4. Design the prompt
        ↓
5. Define output requirements
        ↓
6. Generate AI output
        ↓
7. Check scientific accuracy
        ↓
8. Check instruction following
        ↓
9. Check educational / task suitability
        ↓
10. Identify hallucinations and assumptions
        ↓
11. Revise
        ↓
12. Re-evaluate
        ↓
13. Document the final version
42. Physics AI Quality Checklist

Before accepting an AI-generated Physics response, ask:

Scientific
 Are the Physics concepts correct?
 Are the equations correct?
 Are the variables correctly defined?
 Are the units correct?
 Are signs and directions correct?
 Are assumptions valid?
 Is the physical interpretation correct?
Reasoning
 Does the conclusion follow from the reasoning?
 Are important intermediate steps valid?
 Are unsupported assumptions identified?
 Is the result physically reasonable?
AI Reliability
 Did the model follow the instructions?
 Did it use the supplied information?
 Did it invent unsupported information?
 Did it express uncertainty appropriately?
 Did it remain within scope?
Educational Quality
 Is the explanation appropriate for the learner?
 Is the terminology appropriate?
 Does it address likely misconceptions?
 Is the explanation clear?
 Does it promote understanding rather than memorization?
43. Minimum Acceptance Standard

A Physics AI output should not be considered acceptable merely because it is fluent.

A minimum acceptable response should satisfy:

Scientific Accuracy
        AND
Instruction Following
        AND
Relevant Context
        AND
Appropriate Audience Fit
        AND
No Critical Unsupported Claims

If any critical requirement fails, the response should be reviewed or revised.

44. Final Principle

The goal of Physics AI is not to make AI sound like a Physics expert.

The goal is to make AI useful, reliable, evaluatable, and scientifically responsible when performing Physics-related tasks.

The most important professional mindset is:

Generate with AI. Evaluate with criteria. Verify with Physics expertise. Improve through iteration.
