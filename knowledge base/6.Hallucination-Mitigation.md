# Hallucination Mitigation

> A practical framework for reducing, detecting, evaluating, and responding to unsupported or fabricated information in Generative AI outputs, with particular emphasis on Physics and AI evaluation workflows.

**Status:** Active Development  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Domain:** Generative AI | Prompt Engineering | Physics AI | AI Evaluation  
**Author:** Muhammad Shakhawat Hossain

---

# 1. Purpose

Generative AI systems can produce responses that sound confident and plausible even when the information is unsupported, incomplete, or incorrect.

This is commonly referred to as **AI hallucination**.

For Physics-related applications, hallucinations can be particularly problematic because an incorrect:

- equation
- physical principle
- numerical value
- unit
- scientific claim
- experimental result
- source
- interpretation

may appear convincing to a non-expert.

The purpose of this document is to provide a practical framework for reducing and evaluating hallucination risk.

The central principle is:

> **Do not judge an AI response by how confidently or fluently it is written. Judge it by whether its claims are supported and scientifically valid.**

---

# 2. What Is an AI Hallucination?

In practical Prompt Engineering terms, hallucination refers to an AI-generated claim that is presented as information but is not adequately supported by the available evidence or context.

Examples may include:

- Inventing a fact.
- Inventing a source or citation.
- Providing an incorrect equation.
- Giving a fabricated numerical value.
- Claiming that an experiment produced a result when no result was provided.
- Filling an information gap with an unsupported assumption.
- Presenting uncertain information as certain.

Hallucination is therefore primarily a **reliability problem**.

# 3. Why Hallucinations Matter in Physics

Physics requires precise relationships between:

- quantities
- units
- equations
- assumptions
- measurements
- physical conditions
- mathematical reasoning

A small error can change the meaning of an answer.

For example:

```text
Incorrect equation
      ↓
Incorrect calculation
      ↓
Incorrect numerical result
      ↓
Incorrect physical interpretation

4. The Hallucination Risk Model

A useful way to think about hallucination risk is:

Hallucination Risk
        |
        +-- Missing Information
        |
        +-- Ambiguous Instructions
        |
        +-- Unsupported Claims
        |
        +-- Weak Source Grounding
        |
        +-- Complex Reasoning
        |
        +-- Model Uncertainty
        |
        +-- Overconfident Output

Risk generally increases when the model must provide information that has not been supplied or cannot be reliably inferred.

5. Common Causes of Hallucination
5.1 Missing Context

The model does not have enough information to answer accurately.

Example:

Calculate the acceleration.

without providing the required physical information.

5.2 Ambiguous Input

The question has multiple possible interpretations.

Example:

"Find the force on the object."

without specifying the relevant system or conditions.

5.3 Unsupported Assumptions

The model silently assumes information that was not provided.

For example:

Assuming the surface is frictionless.

when the problem never states that.

5.4 Overgeneralization

The model applies a relationship outside the conditions where it is valid.

5.5 Fabricated Sources

The model may produce citations, papers, authors, URLs, or references that were not verified.

5.6 False Numerical Precision

The model may provide a precise-looking value even when the available information does not justify that precision.

5.7 Confident Language

Phrases such as:

"Clearly..."
"It is well established that..."
"According to..."
"Research shows..."

can create an impression of certainty even when the claim has not been verified.

6. Hallucination Categories

For AI evaluation, it is useful to classify failures.

Category	Description	Example
Fabricated Fact	Claim not supported by available evidence	Invented scientific fact
Fabricated Source	Nonexistent or unverified citation	Invented research paper
Unsupported Inference	Conclusion goes beyond evidence	Claiming a result not established by data
Incorrect Physics	Scientifically incorrect statement	Wrong physical law
Incorrect Equation	Invalid or misapplied equation	Wrong relationship between variables
Numerical Error	Incorrect calculation	Incorrect final value
Unit Error	Incorrect or missing unit	Wrong SI unit
Assumption Error	Unstated or unjustified assumption	Assuming zero friction
Context Error	Misinterpreting the supplied information	Using the wrong condition
Overconfidence	Presenting uncertainty as certainty	No qualification despite insufficient evidence
7. The Core Mitigation Principle

The strongest general strategy is:

Provide Evidence
      ↓
Define Scope
      ↓
Specify Constraints
      ↓
Require Uncertainty
      ↓
Generate
      ↓
Verify

Prompt Engineering can reduce hallucination risk, but prompting alone cannot guarantee factual correctness.

8. Ground the Model in Reliable Information

When reliable source material is available, provide it as context.

For example:

SOURCE INFORMATION:

[Insert verified information]

TASK:

Answer the question using only the information supported
by the supplied source.

RULE:

Do not invent missing information.

This helps establish a boundary between:

Available evidence

and

Unsupported generation.

9. Source-Grounded Prompt Template

Use this reusable template when the task should remain grounded in supplied information.

ROLE:
Act as a careful domain expert.

SOURCE MATERIAL:
[Insert trusted source material]

TASK:
[Describe task]

GROUNDING RULES:
1. Use the supplied source as the primary evidence.
2. Do not invent facts that are absent from the source.
3. Distinguish source-supported information from inference.
4. Identify information gaps.
5. State assumptions explicitly.
6. If the source is insufficient, say so.

OUTPUT:
[Required format]
10. Information-Gap Detection

A strong AI workflow should allow the model to say:

"The available information is insufficient to determine this."

Instead of forcing an answer.

Use:

If the information required to answer the question is not provided:

1. Identify the missing information.
2. Explain why it is needed.
3. State what can still be concluded.
4. Do not invent a value.

This is especially important for numerical Physics problems.

11. Fact vs Inference vs Assumption

AI outputs should distinguish between different levels of support.

Fact

Directly supported by the provided information.

Inference

A conclusion reasonably derived from the available information.

Assumption

An additional condition introduced to complete the task.

Unknown

Something that cannot be determined from the available information.

A useful structure is:

FACTS
↓
INFERENCES
↓
ASSUMPTIONS
↓
UNKNOWN INFORMATION
12. Physics Grounding Checklist

Before accepting an AI-generated Physics response, verify:

 Physical principles are correct.
 Equations are correct.
 Equations are used under appropriate conditions.
 Variables are defined correctly.
 Units are correct.
 Numerical calculations are correct.
 Signs are correct.
 Directions are correct.
 Assumptions are stated.
 The final interpretation matches the calculation.
 Unsupported claims are absent or clearly labeled.
13. Equation Verification

Do not assume an equation is correct simply because it looks familiar.

For important equations, check:

Is the equation scientifically valid?
Does it apply to the stated situation?
Are all variables defined?
Are the units consistent?
Are required conditions satisfied?

For example:

Equation
   ↓
Physical Conditions
   ↓
Variable Definitions
   ↓
Dimensional / Unit Check
   ↓
Application
14. Dimensional Reasoning as a Safety Check

Units can provide an additional check on AI-generated Physics.

For example, if an equation claims:

quantity = force / time

verify whether the resulting dimensions correspond to the claimed quantity.

Dimensional analysis does not prove that an equation is physically correct, but it can reveal many invalid relationships.

15. Numerical Verification

AI-generated numerical calculations should be checked independently when accuracy matters.

Useful checks include:

Recalculate independently.
Check arithmetic.
Check units.
Check significant figures where relevant.
Check order of magnitude.
Check sign.
Check whether the result is physically plausible.

Use:

AI Calculation
      ↓
Independent Verification
      ↓
Physical Reasonableness Check
16. Source Verification

When an AI provides a source, do not assume that the citation is genuine.

Check:

Author
Title
Publication
Year
DOI, when applicable
URL
Whether the source actually contains the claimed information

A citation that looks professional is not necessarily a real or relevant citation.

17. Citation Safety Rule

For important scientific work:

A citation should be treated as a claim requiring verification, not as proof by itself.

The relevant question is:

Does the cited source actually support the statement being made?

18. Uncertainty-Aware Prompting

When certainty matters, explicitly instruct the model to communicate uncertainty.

Example:

For each important claim, distinguish between:

- Directly supported
- Reasonable inference
- Assumption
- Uncertain
- Unsupported

This creates a more transparent output.

19. Confidence Is Not Evidence

AI-generated confidence should not be treated as evidence.

For example:

"This is definitely correct."

does not establish correctness.

A stronger evaluation approach is:

Claim
↓
Evidence
↓
Verification
↓
Conclusion

not:

Claim
↓
Confidence
↓
Acceptance
20. Hallucination Detection Prompt

Use the following template to evaluate AI-generated content.

Act as a factual reliability evaluator.

SOURCE MATERIAL:
[Insert source]

AI OUTPUT:
[Insert AI response]

Analyze every significant factual claim.

Classify each claim as:

1. Supported by source
2. Reasonable inference
3. Unsupported
4. Contradicted by source
5. Cannot be determined

For each claim, provide:

- Claim
- Classification
- Supporting evidence
- Explanation
- Recommended action

Do not assume that fluent or confident language indicates correctness.

OUTPUT:

| Claim | Classification | Evidence | Explanation | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |

## Overall Reliability

[Assessment]

## Critical Issues

[List]

## Information Gaps

[List]

22. Hallucination Mitigation Through Prompt Constraints

Useful constraints include:

- Use only the supplied source.
- Do not invent missing values.
- Identify unsupported claims.
- State assumptions explicitly.
- Identify information gaps.
- Do not fabricate citations.
- Ask for clarification when necessary.
- Distinguish fact from inference.

However, constraints should be relevant to the task.

Avoid adding every possible restriction to every prompt.

23. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

When a task requires external knowledge, a retrieval-based workflow can provide relevant information to the model.

Conceptually:

User Question
      ↓
Retrieve Relevant Information
      ↓
Filter / Rank Context
      ↓
Provide Context to Model
      ↓
Generate Response
      ↓
Evaluate Grounding

The goal is to reduce reliance on unsupported model-generated information.

RAG does not automatically eliminate hallucinations.

Poor retrieval or irrelevant context can still produce incorrect answers.

24. RAG Grounding Checklist

When using retrieved information, verify:

 The retrieved source is relevant.
 The retrieved information actually supports the answer.
 Important claims are grounded.
 Conflicting sources are identified.
 Missing information is acknowledged.
 The model does not add unsupported details.
 Source attribution is preserved where required.
25. Context Quality Matters

Providing more information does not necessarily improve reliability.

Poor context may be:

Irrelevant
Contradictory
Outdated
Incorrect
Ambiguous
Excessively long

A useful principle is:

Relevant, reliable context is more valuable than large amounts of unfiltered context.

26. Contradictory Sources

If supplied sources disagree, the model should not silently choose one.

Use:

If sources conflict:

1. Identify the conflict.
2. Present the relevant positions.
3. Determine whether one source has stronger authority.
4. Explain the basis for the decision.
5. If the conflict cannot be resolved, state that explicitly.
27. Out-of-Scope Detection

Hallucination risk increases when the model attempts tasks outside the intended scope.

A useful prompt instruction is:

If the request is outside the available evidence or defined scope,
identify it as out of scope rather than generating an unsupported answer.

This is especially useful for specialized Physics AI systems.

28. Clarification Before Generation

When missing information materially affects the answer, asking a clarification question may be better than guessing.

Example:

Missing:
Coefficient of friction

Effect:
Cannot determine the frictional force accurately.

Action:
Request the coefficient or state a clearly labeled assumption.

The model should not silently choose a value.

29. Hallucination Severity Framework

Not every unsupported statement has the same importance.

Critical

Could fundamentally mislead the user or invalidate the result.

Examples:

Incorrect fundamental law
Dangerous laboratory instruction
Fabricated scientific evidence
Completely incorrect final result
Major

Substantially affects understanding or correctness.

Examples:

Incorrect equation
Important misconception
Incorrect assumption
Minor

Does not substantially change the main conclusion.

Examples:

Small wording issue
Minor omission
Nonessential imprecision
30. Hallucination Triage

When multiple problems exist, prioritize them.

Critical Errors
      ↓
Major Errors
      ↓
Minor Errors
      ↓
Stylistic Improvements

Do not spend more time correcting formatting than correcting a scientifically incorrect equation.

31. Human-in-the-Loop Verification

For important Physics workflows:

AI
↓
Automated Checks
↓
Human SME
↓
Approved Output

Automated checks may detect:

Missing units
Formatting problems
Required fields
Obvious inconsistencies
Duplicate content

A Physics SME may need to evaluate:

Scientific correctness
Conceptual validity
Appropriate assumptions
Educational usefulness
Subtle reasoning errors
32. Hallucination Mitigation for Student Feedback

When AI evaluates a student response:

Do not allow the model to invent what the student "must have meant."

Instead:

Student Evidence
      ↓
Observed Reasoning
      ↓
Possible Interpretation
      ↓
Confidence / Uncertainty
      ↓
Feedback

Use language such as:

"Your response appears to suggest..."

rather than presenting an unsupported interpretation as fact.

Misconception risk

34. Hallucination Mitigation for AI Training Data

When creating Physics AI training examples:

Generate
   ↓
Review
   ↓
Verify Physics
   ↓
Check Labels
   ↓
Remove Unsupported Claims
   ↓
Approve

Do not automatically treat generated examples as ground truth.

35. Hallucination Mitigation for AI Evaluation Data

Evaluation datasets should contain verified reference answers or clearly defined scoring criteria.

Check:

Reference correctness
Label consistency
Physics accuracy
Ambiguity
Coverage
Edge cases
Misconceptions
Out-of-scope examples

A flawed evaluation dataset can incorrectly reward bad AI behavior.

36. Prompt-Level Mitigation vs System-Level Mitigation

It is important to distinguish between these.

Prompt-Level Mitigation

Examples:

Source grounding
Explicit constraints
Uncertainty instructions
Output structure
Information-gap detection
System-Level Mitigation

Examples:

Retrieval systems
Source validation
Automated checks
Human review
Evaluation pipelines
Monitoring
Dataset quality control

Prompting is only one layer of reliability.

Prompt
  +
Context
  +
Retrieval
  +
Validation
  +
Evaluation
  +
Human Oversight

provides a stronger reliability framework than prompting alone.

37. Hallucination Testing Workflow

A prompt or system designed to reduce hallucination should be tested.

Baseline Prompt
      ↓
Generate Outputs
      ↓
Identify Hallucinations
      ↓
Classify Failures
      ↓

      ↓
Retest
      ↓
Compare Results

Record:

Number of unsupported claims
Types of errors
Severity
Frequency
Whether mitigation reduced the errors
38. Adversarial / Edge-Case Testing

Test whether the model hallucinates when:

Information is missing.
The question is ambiguous.
The source contains conflicting information.
The user asks for an impossible calculation.
A false premise is included.
The model is asked for a nonexistent source.
The input contains misleading information.
The task goes outside the defined domain.

A reliable system should recognize these conditions rather than confidently filling the gaps.

39. False Premise Detection

A user question may contain an incorrect assumption.

Example structure:

QUESTION:
[Question containing possible false premise]

TASK:
Determine whether the premise is scientifically valid before answering.

If the premise is incorrect:

1. Identify the incorrect assumption.
2. Explain the relevant Physics.
3. Correct the premise.
4. Then answer the underlying question if possible.

This is often better than accepting the user's premise automatically.

40. Do Not Force an Answer

One of the most important hallucination-mitigation principles is:

An accurate "insufficient information" response is better than an invented answer.

A system should be allowed to say:

"I cannot determine this from the information provided."
"This requires an additional assumption."
"The source does not establish this claim."
"This should be verified."
"The question is ambiguous."

These are valid outcomes.

44. Physics AI Hallucination Risk Matrix
Situation	Risk	Recommended Mitigation
Well-defined basic concept	Lower	Standard verification
Complex multi-step calculation	Medium	Independent calculation check
Missing numerical data	High	Identify information gap
Ambiguous problem	High	Clarify assumptions
Research claim	High	Verify source
Experimental result	High	Ground in actual data
Generated citation	High	Verify citation
AI training example	High	SME review
High-stakes educational assessment	High	Human evaluation
Safety-related Physics instruction	Very High	Expert verification

Risk level should be determined by both the likelihood of error and the consequence of being wrong.

45. Core Principles

The following principles summarize this document:

Principle 1

Ground important claims in reliable evidence.

Principle 2

Do not allow missing information to become invented information.

Principle 3

Separate facts, inference, assumptions, and unknowns.

Principle 4

Verify Physics, not just language.

Principle 5

Treat equations and numerical results as claims requiring verification.

Principle 6

Do not treat confidence as evidence.

Principle 7

Allow the AI to acknowledge uncertainty.

Principle 8

Use structured evaluation for important outputs.

Principle 9

Use human SME review when the consequences of error are significant.

Principle 10

Treat hallucination mitigation as a workflow, not a single prompt instruction.

46. Final Professional Principle

The objective of hallucination mitigation is not to make an AI system appear perfectly confident.

It is to make the system more transparent about what it knows, what it infers, what it assumes, and what it cannot establish.

For Physics AI, the professional standard is:

Evidence before confidence.
Verification before acceptance.
Physics expertise before final approval.
