# Case Study 1 — The "Bad Prompt" in a Physics Classroom

## Original Lecture Example

The instructor demonstrated that a vague prompt asking AI to write an announcement email produced an overly promotional response containing fabricated information.

---

## Physics Classroom Equivalent

### Vague Prompt

> "Write an email to parents about our new Physics laboratory starting next month."

---

### Likely AI Output

> "We are thrilled to announce our state-of-the-art Physics laboratory featuring cutting-edge equipment and exciting hands-on experiments designed to unlock your child's scientific potential."

---

### Why This Fails

This output does not reflect my writing style or classroom reality.

Problems include:

- It uses exaggerated marketing language that I would never use.
- It assumes expensive laboratory equipment without evidence.
- It introduces claims that were never provided.
- It sounds like an advertisement instead of communication from an experienced teacher.

---

### Prompt Engineering Lesson

The problem is not the AI model.

The problem is that the prompt failed to provide:

- writing style
- audience expectations
- factual context
- behavioural constraints

Without this information, the model relied on statistically common announcement patterns.

# Case Study 2 — AI Hallucination in Physics Education

Hallucination is particularly dangerous in scientific and educational contexts.

Unlike marketing copy, inaccurate educational content can create misconceptions or even introduce safety risks.

Examples include:

| Hallucination Risk | Physics Example | Potential Consequence |
|-------------------|-----------------|-----------------------|
| Inventing laboratory equipment | AI assumes a laser interferometer exists when the experiment actually uses a simple pendulum | Students receive incorrect laboratory instructions |
| Inventing experimental data | AI generates sample measurements before the experiment has been conducted | Students misunderstand scientific investigation |
| Inventing formulas | AI produces an incorrect equation that appears mathematically plausible | Reinforces scientific misconceptions |
| Inventing safety procedures | AI assumes laboratory safety protocols that were never provided | Could create unsafe classroom practices |

---

### Lesson for My Own Prompt Design

Whenever I use AI to generate:

- laboratory instructions,
- lesson plans,
- experiment descriptions,
- assessment materials,
- or classroom communications,

I must explicitly provide:

- the real equipment,
- verified formulas,
- actual procedures,
- confirmed dates,
- accurate scientific terminology,

and instruct the model **never to invent missing information.**

For Physics educators, hallucinations are more than writing mistakes—they can affect scientific accuracy, student learning, and laboratory safety.

# Case Study 3 — Learning a Teacher's Writing Style

## Original Lecture Analogy

The instructor explained that learning to write like William Faulkner requires studying examples, practising, comparing, and refining rather than issuing a single prompt.

---

## Physics Equivalent

Suppose I want AI to explain Physics in the style of Richard Feynman.

A prompt such as:

> "Explain Momentum like Richard Feynman."

is far too vague.

The AI may imitate a few surface characteristics but cannot reliably reproduce Feynman's reasoning process.

A better engineering approach would be:

1. Provide several authentic excerpts from Feynman's writing.
2. Ask the AI to identify recurring communication patterns.
3. List those patterns explicitly.
4. Apply the same patterns to a new Physics topic.
5. Compare the generated explanation with authentic examples.
6. Refine the prompt iteratively.

This mirrors exactly how I should build prompts for my own teaching voice.

Instead of asking:

> "Write like an experienced Physics teacher."

I should provide:

- authentic classroom emails,
- lesson introductions,
- assessment instructions,
- explanation samples,

allowing the model to learn my actual communication style through examples.

# Educational AI Implications

Prompt engineering becomes even more important when AI is used in educational environments.

Educational content must prioritize:

- factual accuracy,
- conceptual clarity,
- pedagogical sequencing,
- audience appropriateness,
- scientific correctness,
- student safety.

Poor prompts may generate:

- incorrect explanations,
- misleading analogies,
- invented scientific facts,
- ineffective teaching materials.

Unlike casual conversations with AI, educational outputs have long-term consequences for student understanding.

# Best Practices for AI-Assisted Physics Writing

Based on this lesson, I will follow these principles whenever designing prompts for Physics education.

- Clearly define the intended audience.
- Specify my writing style and teaching philosophy.
- Provide authentic writing samples whenever possible.
- Include only verified facts.
- Explicitly prohibit AI from inventing formulas, equipment, procedures, or safety instructions.
- Define the desired output structure.
- Review every AI-generated response for scientific accuracy before use.

# Final Insight

This lesson demonstrates that prompt engineering is not simply about obtaining better AI responses.

It is fundamentally about reducing uncertainty.

Every missing instruction increases the likelihood that an AI system will generate generic, inaccurate, or fabricated information.

For Physics education, where scientific precision and student safety are essential, prompt engineering becomes a professional teaching competency rather than merely a technical skill.

As an aspiring **Physics AI Subject Matter Expert**, I believe that designing high-quality prompts is inseparable from designing high-quality educational experiences.
